================================================================================
FIGURE 5 DESIGN DOCUMENT
Circuit Gain Modulation Generates a Continuum of Mate Preference
================================================================================

Authors' note: This figure bridges the structural/functional analysis of the
multimodal courtship circuit (Fig 6) with the evolutionary variation in mate
preference documented by Ryba et al. (2025). It asks: given the wiring of
the male CNS connectome, what is the parameter space of courtship behavior
that gain modulation allows?

================================================================================
I.  FIGURE NARRATIVE
================================================================================

The central premise of Ryba et al. (2025) is that behavioral evolution in
Drosophila mate preference operates through a surprisingly simple mechanism:
variation in the gain of a single ascending inhibitory pathway (mAL GABAergic
neurons) connecting peripheral contact chemosensory neurons (Ppk23+) to the
courtship command neurons (P1). Selective strains (CO13N) have strong
mAL-mediated inhibition of P1; promiscuous strains (CS) have weak inhibition.
Peripheral detection of the inhibitory pheromone 7-tricosene is conserved
across strains -- the variation is entirely central.

This finding raises a deeper question that the connectome can uniquely address:
is the mAL pathway the ONLY viable locus for this kind of evolutionary tuning,
or does the circuit contain many potential "tuning knobs"? If mAL is
special, what makes it special? If there are other knobs, why hasn't
evolution turned them?

Figure 5 answers this by computing the full gain modulation landscape of the
courtship circuit. We systematically vary the gain (synaptic efficacy) at
every intermediate neuron type in the circuit and measure how these changes
affect the relative activation of courtship decision neurons by conspecific
versus heterospecific stimulus patterns. The result is a quantitative map of
the circuit's "evolvability" -- which gain changes efficiently shift mate
selectivity, which changes break courtship entirely, and which changes are
functionally neutral.

The figure builds the following argument:

  (1) The circuit contains many potential gain modulation sites, but only a
      small subset have high LEVERAGE over mate selectivity (Panel A).

  (2) Among high-leverage nodes, there is a qualitative distinction between
      "selectivity switches" (steep response curves: small gain changes
      produce large preference shifts) and "vigor modulators" (changes
      that scale overall courtship drive without affecting selectivity)
      (Panel B).

  (3) When two nodes are varied simultaneously, the preference landscape
      reveals ridges, valleys, and basins that constrain the space of
      achievable phenotypes. Not all phenotypic states are equally
      accessible from any starting point (Panel C).

  (4) The 16 strains characterized by Ryba et al. are predicted to map
      onto a low-dimensional manifold within this landscape, consistent
      with the experimental finding that behavioral variation is largely
      one-dimensional (Panel D).

  (5) The mAL-like inhibitory neurons identified by Ryba et al. occupy a
      unique position in parameter space: they have the highest ratio of
      "useful variation range" (range over which selectivity changes
      smoothly) to "total variation range" (range before courtship
      breaks). This makes them ROBUST SELECTIVITY KNOBS -- the ideal
      substrate for evolutionary variation (Panel E).

  (6) This robustness asymmetry arises from a fundamental E/I asymmetry:
      inhibitory gain modulation selectively affects preference, while
      excitatory gain modulation primarily affects courtship vigor. The
      circuit's neurotransmitter architecture thus channels evolutionary
      variation into the inhibitory pathway (Panel F).

  (7) Finally, a graph-theoretic analysis of "evolutionary accessibility"
      shows that the selective-to-promiscuous transition requires
      single-node gain changes in the inhibitory pathway, while other
      phenotypic transitions (e.g., reversed preference) would require
      coordinated multi-node changes. The circuit architecture makes
      the observed variation EASY and the unobserved variation HARD
      (Panel G).


================================================================================
II.  PANEL SPECIFICATIONS
================================================================================

----------
PANEL A: The gain parameter space -- Leverage heatmap
----------

Question: Which neurons in the circuit have the greatest influence over
mate selectivity when their gain is modulated?

Data and computation:
  We work with the signed adjacency matrix W (post-normalized, signed by
  neurotransmitter identity) already exported to
  /combinatorial_signal_flow/data/adj_matrix_post.mtx and sign-corrected
  via NetworkLoader.sign_matrix(). The 7 sensory channels (DA1, VA1v,
  VA1d, auditory, visual, ppk23, ppk25) are encoded as binary input
  vectors via ChannelEncoder.

  Define "conspecific stimulus" as the combination of channels active
  during courtship of a D. melanogaster female:
    S_con = ppk23 + ppk25 + DA1 + visual + auditory
  (cVA pheromones activate DA1; visual and auditory are courtship-song
  and visual-motion cues; ppk23/ppk25 detect cuticular hydrocarbons.)

  Define "heterospecific stimulus" (or male-like stimulus) as:
    S_het = ppk23 + VA1v
  (7-tricosene activates VA1v; ppk23 detects male CHCs. Ryba et al.
  show that the male-specific 7-T pheromone is the key inhibitory cue.)

  For each intermediate neuron type n_i in the network (excluding sensory
  neurons and target decision neurons), define a gain parameter g_i that
  multiplies all outgoing synaptic weights from n_i:
    W_modified[n_i, :] = g_i * W[n_i, :]

  LEVERAGE SCORE for neuron n_i with respect to channel c:
    L(n_i, c) = |d(PI) / d(g_i)|  evaluated at g_i = 1.0

  where PI is the Preference Index:
    PI = [A(S_con, target) - A(S_het, target)] / [A(S_con, target) + A(S_het, target)]

  and A(S, target) is the activation at the target neurons (P1 cluster, or
  aSP-f / aSP-g as proxies for courtship drive) after propagating stimulus
  S through the gain-modified network for 3 steps with sustained input and
  sigmoid_rectified nonlinearity.

  Numerically, compute the derivative via central finite differences:
    L(n_i, c) ~ |PI(g_i = 1.01) - PI(g_i = 0.99)| / 0.02

  This is computed for each intermediate neuron type (there are ~15,000
  types in the male CNS, but most have zero connectivity to the courtship
  circuit -- we pre-filter to types that appear in the top-1000 strongest
  paths from any sensory channel to any target, which is ~500 types).

  The leverage score is computed separately for each target group (aSP-f,
  aSP-g, vAB3_downstream, PPN1_downstream) to capture target-specific
  effects.

Visualization:
  Heatmap with rows = intermediate neuron types (sorted by maximum
  leverage across all targets), columns = target groups. Color = log10
  of leverage score (diverging colormap, with high-leverage neurons
  highlighted in red/orange). Right-margin annotation bars indicate:
    (i)   Neurotransmitter identity (E/I color)
    (ii)  Circuit layer (hop distance from sensory input)
    (iii) Whether the neuron is in the mAL cluster

  Highlight the top 10 highest-leverage neurons with labels.

Expected results:
  - The mAL_m subtypes (mAL_m1, mAL_m3b, mAL_m3c, mAL_m8, mAL_m9) should
    appear among the top 10-20 highest-leverage neurons, confirming the
    Ryba et al. finding from a purely connectomic perspective.
  - The AN09B017 family (glutamatergic interneurons that appeared as key
    intermediaries in the ppk interaction investigation) should also show
    high leverage, but predominantly on VIGOR rather than SELECTIVITY.
  - Most neurons in the network have near-zero leverage -- the circuit's
    behavior is controlled by a small set of high-leverage nodes.

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  This panel provides a connectome-derived PREDICTION that the mAL neurons
  are among the most powerful gain modulation sites in the circuit, prior
  to and independent of the experimental identification by Ryba et al.
  The convergence of experimental and computational identification
  validates both approaches.


----------
PANEL B: Single-node gain sweeps -- Selectivity switches vs. vigor modulators
----------

Question: For the highest-leverage neurons, how does varying their gain
from 0 (silenced) to 2x (enhanced) change mate preference?

Data and computation:
  For the top 10 highest-leverage neurons identified in Panel A, sweep
  gain g_i over the range [0.0, 0.1, 0.2, ..., 1.8, 1.9, 2.0] (21
  values). At each gain value, propagate both S_con and S_het through
  the modified network (3 steps, sustained, sigmoid_rectified with
  beta=5.0). Compute:

    PI(g_i) = [A_con(g_i) - A_het(g_i)] / [A_con(g_i) + A_het(g_i)]

  where A_con and A_het are mean activations across target neurons in the
  P1 cluster (or aSP-f/aSP-g, depending on the analysis target).

  Also compute:
    VIGOR(g_i) = A_con(g_i) + A_het(g_i)
  (total courtship drive, regardless of preference.)

  SELECTIVITY SENSITIVITY = max |dPI/dg| over the sweep range
  VIGOR SENSITIVITY = max |dVIGOR/dg| over the sweep range

  A "selectivity switch" is a neuron with high selectivity sensitivity
  relative to vigor sensitivity (steep PI curve, flat VIGOR curve).
  A "vigor modulator" is the opposite (flat PI, steep VIGOR).

Visualization:
  Two-row panel layout (one row per metric):
    Row 1: Stacked line plots of PI(g) for each neuron (colored by
    neurotransmitter identity: inhibitory = blue, excitatory = red).
    Y-axis = Preference Index [-1, +1]. X-axis = gain factor [0, 2].
    Horizontal dashed line at PI=0 (no preference). Vertical dashed
    line at g=1 (baseline). Gray shaded region marks the "useful range"
    where |dPI/dg| > threshold.

    Row 2: Corresponding VIGOR(g) curves.

  Alternative: Small-multiples faceted by neuron, with PI and VIGOR
  as two traces on the same plot (dual y-axis or normalized to [0,1]).

  Inset scatter: selectivity sensitivity vs. vigor sensitivity for all
  10 neurons, with quadrants labeled "selectivity switch" (top-left),
  "vigor modulator" (bottom-right), "both" (top-right), "neither"
  (bottom-left). Points colored by E/I identity.

Expected results:
  - INHIBITORY neurons (especially mAL subtypes) should show steep PI
    curves with relatively flat VIGOR curves: changing their gain shifts
    WHAT the animal prefers without changing HOW MUCH it courts.
    This is the "selectivity switch" phenotype.
    Mechanistically: mAL neurons are GABAergic and sit in the ascending
    pathway from ppk23 to P1. Increasing mAL gain increases inhibition
    of P1 by 7-T-responsive input, making the animal more selective.
    Decreasing mAL gain releases P1 from this inhibition, making the
    animal more promiscuous. But total courtship drive is maintained
    because the excitatory pathways (olfactory, visual, auditory) are
    unaffected.

  - EXCITATORY neurons (e.g., AN09B017 family, P1 subtypes) should show
    steep VIGOR curves with flatter PI curves: changing their gain changes
    HOW MUCH the animal courts without changing WHAT it prefers.
    Mechanistically: these neurons amplify all sensory signals equally,
    so changing their gain scales the entire courtship response up or
    down without differentially affecting conspecific vs. heterospecific
    activation.

  - This E/I asymmetry is the key prediction: the neurotransmitter
    identity of a gain-modulated node determines WHETHER the effect is
    on selectivity or vigor.

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  Ryba et al. show that "depolarizing mAL in a promiscuous background
  makes flies selective; hyperpolarizing mAL in a selective background
  makes flies promiscuous." This corresponds exactly to the "selectivity
  switch" phenotype: the PI curve has a steep transition centered around
  the baseline gain, and the direction of the shift matches the
  prediction from our gain sweep.


----------
PANEL C: Two-dimensional gain landscape -- Contour plots
----------

Question: What does the preference landscape look like when two
nodes are varied simultaneously?

Data and computation:
  Select 2-3 pairs of high-leverage neurons that represent biologically
  meaningful combinations:
    Pair 1: mAL_m1 (inhibitory) x AN09B017f (excitatory)
      -- The "selectivity vs. vigor" axes
    Pair 2: mAL_m1 (inhibitory) x mAL_m8 (inhibitory)
      -- Two inhibitory nodes in the same pathway
    Pair 3: AN09B017f (excitatory) x AN09B017g (excitatory)
      -- Two excitatory nodes in the same pathway

  For each pair, create a 21x21 grid of gain values (g1, g2) in
  [0.0, 2.0] x [0.0, 2.0]. At each grid point, modify both neurons'
  outgoing weights simultaneously and propagate both S_con and S_het.
  Compute PI(g1, g2).

  Also compute:
    VIGOR(g1, g2) = A_con(g1, g2) + A_het(g1, g2)
    FUNCTIONAL(g1, g2) = 1 if VIGOR > threshold, 0 otherwise
      (whether courtship function is preserved)

  Compute the gradient field of PI(g1, g2) on the landscape:
    nabla PI = (dPI/dg1, dPI/dg2)

  The "path of least resistance" is the streamline of steepest ascent
  in the PI landscape that starts from the baseline (g1=1, g2=1) and
  moves toward the direction of maximal preference change per unit
  gain change. This is computed by gradient ascent:
    (g1, g2) <- (g1, g2) + eta * nabla PI

  where eta is a small step size.

Visualization:
  Three contour plots side-by-side (one per neuron pair):
    X-axis = gain at node 1, Y-axis = gain at node 2.
    Color = PI value (diverging colormap: blue = preference for
    heterospecific, white = no preference, red = preference for
    conspecific).
    Contour lines at PI = {-0.5, -0.25, 0, 0.25, 0.5}.
    Black dashed lines mark the boundary of the "functional zone"
    (where VIGOR > threshold).
    White arrows show the gradient field (direction and magnitude
    of steepest PI change).
    A highlighted path shows the "path of least resistance" from
    baseline.
    A star marks the baseline (1,1).

Expected results:
  - Pair 1 (mAL_m1 x AN09B017f): The landscape should show a roughly
    rectangular functional zone with PI varying primarily along the
    mAL_m1 axis (horizontal gradient). The path of least resistance
    should be nearly horizontal -- changing mAL gain alone is the most
    efficient way to shift selectivity. This confirms that the inhibitory
    pathway is the "easy" evolutionary target.

  - Pair 2 (mAL_m1 x mAL_m8): The landscape should show a ridge along
    the diagonal (changing both mAL subtypes in the same direction has
    redundant effects) with the path of least resistance running along
    the ridge. This predicts that evolutionary changes in the mAL pathway
    should affect multiple mAL subtypes coordinately (consistent with
    Ryba et al.'s finding that the entire mAL population shows strain
    variation).

  - Pair 3 (AN09B017f x AN09B017g): The landscape should show a narrow
    functional zone with PI varying weakly -- changing excitatory gain
    at two nodes simultaneously primarily affects vigor, not selectivity.
    The functional zone boundary should be close to the baseline,
    indicating fragility.

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  The landscape geometry provides a mechanistic explanation for why the
  mAL pathway is the substrate of evolutionary variation: it sits on a
  long, flat ridge in gain space where large movements produce smooth
  changes in selectivity without breaking courtship. Other pathways sit
  on narrow peaks where small movements are catastrophic.


----------
PANEL D: Mapping strains onto the landscape
----------

Question: Where do the 16 Ryba et al. strains fall in the gain landscape?

Data and computation:
  Ryba et al. characterize 16 D. melanogaster strains along a continuous
  selectivity axis, from CO13N (most selective) to CS (most promiscuous).
  They measure: (i) behavioral preference index (discrimination between
  D. mel and D. sim females), (ii) mAL calcium responses to 7-T, and
  (iii) P1 calcium responses to 7-T.

  From their behavioral data, we extract a 1D ordering of strains by
  preference index. We then ask: what gain parameter values at the mAL
  nodes would produce the observed preference indices in our model?

  For each strain s with observed preference PI_obs(s), solve:
    g_mAL(s) = argmin_g |PI_model(g) - PI_obs(s)|
  where PI_model(g) is the preference index from the Panel B gain sweep
  at mAL neurons. This is a simple 1D root-finding problem.

  Then, for the 2D landscape (Panel C, Pair 1), map each strain to:
    (g_mAL(s), g_exc = 1.0)
  i.e., position it on the mAL axis with excitatory gain at baseline.

  KEY TEST: If behavioral variation maps to a 1D path in gain space,
  then the 16 strains should fall on a line (or narrow band) in the
  2D landscape. The spread perpendicular to this line measures
  "unexplained variation" -- variation that cannot be attributed to
  mAL gain alone.

  We quantify this with a linearity measure:
    R^2 = variance along the first principal component of strain
    positions in 2D gain space / total variance

  If the variation is purely 1D (as Ryba et al. predict), R^2 ~ 1.0.

Visualization:
  Overlay on the Panel C contour plot (Pair 1: mAL x excitatory):
    - 16 labeled points, one per strain, positioned at (g_mAL(s), 1.0).
    - Color gradient from blue (selective) to red (promiscuous).
    - An arrow connecting CO13N to CS shows the "evolutionary trajectory."
    - Inset: 1D projection showing PI_obs vs. g_mAL(s) with a fitted
      monotonic curve. Report R^2.

  IMPORTANT CAVEAT: We do not have the actual per-strain behavioral data
  from Ryba et al. in our connectome dataset. This panel should be
  framed as a PREDICTION: "Given the Ryba et al. strain ordering, the
  model predicts that the following gain values would reproduce the
  observed behavioral variation." If Ryba et al. data becomes available
  for quantitative fitting, the predicted gain values can be compared
  to measured mAL response amplitudes.

Expected results:
  - The 16 strains should map to a monotonic 1D path along the mAL gain
    axis, with no systematic displacement along the excitatory axis.
  - The most selective strain (CO13N) should correspond to g_mAL ~ 1.5-2.0
    (enhanced mAL inhibition), and the most promiscuous strain (CS) should
    correspond to g_mAL ~ 0.3-0.7 (reduced mAL inhibition).
  - The path should lie entirely within the "functional zone" where
    VIGOR > threshold, consistent with all strains producing normal
    courtship (just with different selectivity).

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  Ryba et al. show that their 16 strains form a continuous 1D gradient.
  Our model predicts that this gradient maps to a 1D manifold in gain
  space -- specifically, variation along a single pathway (mAL). This is
  the connectome-level explanation for why "multigenic architecture may
  nevertheless belie a relatively simple neural logic."


----------
PANEL E: Robustness and fragility analysis
----------

Question: Which gain changes preserve courtship function while shifting
selectivity, and which changes break the circuit?

Data and computation:
  For each of the top 20 highest-leverage neurons, define:

  USEFUL RANGE: The range of gain values [g_min, g_max] over which:
    (i)   VIGOR(g) > 0.5 * VIGOR(1.0)  [courtship not abolished]
    (ii)  |dPI/dg| > epsilon           [preference actually changes]
  Width: w_useful = g_max - g_min

  TOTAL RANGE: [0, 2.0] by construction (or the range before activation
  goes to zero / saturates).
  Width: w_total = 2.0

  FRAGILITY INDEX:
    F(n_i) = 1 - (w_useful / w_total)
  High F = fragile (small useful range; most gain changes break courtship).
  Low F = robust (large useful range; the node is a good evolutionary knob).

  SELECTIVITY RANGE: The range of PI values achievable within the useful
  range:
    delta_PI = max(PI(g)) - min(PI(g)) for g in [g_min, g_max]

  EFFICIENCY:
    E(n_i) = delta_PI / w_useful
  High efficiency = large selectivity change per unit gain change.
  This measures how "sensitive" the selectivity knob is.

  ROBUSTNESS-EFFICIENCY PRODUCT:
    REP(n_i) = (1 - F(n_i)) * E(n_i)
  This is the key metric: a good evolutionary knob has BOTH large useful
  range AND high efficiency. The REP measures the "quality" of the knob.

Visualization:
  Main panel: Scatter plot with x-axis = Fragility F(n_i) and y-axis =
  Selectivity Range delta_PI. Points colored by neurotransmitter identity
  (inhibitory = blue, excitatory = red). Point size proportional to
  log(leverage score). Labels for top 5 neurons.

  Quadrants:
    Top-left (low fragility, high selectivity range) = "Ideal evolutionary
    knobs" -- PREDICT mAL neurons here.
    Top-right (high fragility, high selectivity range) = "Dangerous knobs"
    -- powerful but one wrong step breaks things.
    Bottom-left (low fragility, low selectivity range) = "Safe but useless"
    -- robust but don't affect preference.
    Bottom-right (high fragility, low selectivity range) = "Worst of both
    worlds" -- fragile AND don't help.

  Right margin: Bar chart of REP scores for the top 10 neurons, sorted
  descending. Highlight mAL neurons with asterisks.

Expected results:
  - mAL subtypes should cluster in the "ideal evolutionary knob" quadrant
    (top-left): they have large useful range (gain can be varied widely
    without abolishing courtship) AND high selectivity range (preference
    changes substantially across this range).
  - AN09B017 subtypes should fall in the bottom-left quadrant: robust but
    primarily affecting vigor, not selectivity.
  - P1 subtypes should fall in the top-right quadrant: high selectivity
    range but fragile (small gain changes abolish courtship because they
    are the command neurons themselves).

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  The finding that mAL neurons are "ideal evolutionary knobs" explains
  WHY evolution converged on this pathway across multiple independently
  derived strains. The circuit architecture constrains evolution to work
  through the nodes that allow smooth, functional variation.


----------
PANEL F: Excitatory vs. inhibitory gain asymmetry
----------

Question: Does the E/I identity of a gain-modulated node determine
whether the effect is on selectivity or vigor?

Data and computation:
  For all intermediate neurons in the courtship circuit (those with
  nonzero leverage), classify by neurotransmitter:
    Inhibitory: GABA, glycine, histamine (is_inhibitory = TRUE in
    neurotransmitter_mapping.csv)
    Excitatory: acetylcholine, glutamate

  For each neuron, compute:
    SELECTIVITY SENSITIVITY: max |dPI/dg| from the Panel B sweep
    VIGOR SENSITIVITY: max |d(VIGOR)/dg| from the Panel B sweep

  Compute the SELECTIVITY/VIGOR RATIO:
    SVR(n_i) = selectivity sensitivity / vigor sensitivity

  Compare distributions of SVR between inhibitory and excitatory neurons.

  Statistical tests:
    - Wilcoxon rank-sum test on SVR(inhibitory) vs SVR(excitatory)
    - Permutation test: shuffle E/I labels 10,000 times, recompute
      mean SVR difference, compute p-value

Visualization:
  Left panel: Paired box/violin plots of SVR for inhibitory vs. excitatory
  neurons. Y-axis = selectivity/vigor ratio (log scale). Box whiskers show
  IQR, individual points overlaid. Bracket with p-value from Wilcoxon test.

  Right panel: Scatter plot of selectivity sensitivity vs. vigor sensitivity
  for all neurons, with inhibitory (blue) and excitatory (red) as separate
  populations. 95% confidence ellipses for each group. Diagonal line marks
  SVR = 1 (equal sensitivity). Inhibitory neurons should cluster above the
  line (selectivity-dominant); excitatory below (vigor-dominant).

  Inset: Histogram of the permutation test null distribution with the
  observed mean SVR difference marked as a vertical line.

Expected results:
  - Inhibitory neurons should have significantly higher SVR than excitatory
    neurons (p < 0.001), confirming that GABAergic gain modulation
    preferentially affects selectivity.
  - The effect should be strongest for neurons in the ascending ppk23 ->
    mAL -> P1 pathway, which is the specific pathway identified by Ryba.
  - Excitatory neurons should have SVR ~ 1 or < 1, indicating that their
    gain changes affect vigor as much as or more than selectivity.

Biological interpretation:
  This panel provides a circuit-theoretic explanation for WHY inhibitory
  gain is the substrate of evolutionary variation in mate preference:

  (1) Inhibitory neurons create a "gate" in the circuit: they can
      selectively block specific sensory channels (the 7-T pathway)
      without affecting others (olfactory, visual, auditory).

  (2) Excitatory neurons create a "volume knob": they amplify all
      signals passing through them, regardless of identity.

  (3) For selectivity to change, you need a gate, not a volume knob.
      Gates are inhibitory. Therefore, evolution modulates inhibition.

  This argument extends Ryba et al.'s specific finding about mAL to a
  general principle: in any sensory integration circuit, the nodes where
  inhibitory gain modulation has disproportionate effects on stimulus
  discrimination (relative to response magnitude) are the predicted
  substrates of evolutionary variation in stimulus preference.


----------
PANEL G: Evolutionary accessibility network
----------

Question: Which phenotypic transitions are "easy" (accessible via
single-node gain changes) vs. "hard" (requiring coordinated multi-node
changes)?

Data and computation:
  Define a set of behaviorally meaningful PHENOTYPIC STATES:
    P1: "Selective" -- PI > 0.5 (strong conspecific preference)
    P2: "Moderate"  -- 0.1 < PI < 0.5 (weak conspecific preference)
    P3: "Promiscuous" -- -0.1 < PI < 0.1 (no preference)
    P4: "Reversed" -- PI < -0.1 (heterospecific preference)
    P5: "Courtship-off" -- VIGOR < threshold (courtship abolished)

  Build a TRANSITION GRAPH:
    Nodes = phenotypic states P1-P5.
    Directed edges = single-node gain changes that move from one state
    to another.

  For each intermediate neuron n_i and each starting state P_start:
    Sweep gain over [0, 2.0] and record which states are visited.
    If the sweep passes through a boundary between P_start and P_target,
    record an edge (P_start -> P_target) with weight = minimum gain
    change required to cross the boundary.

  ACCESSIBILITY SCORE for transition (P_start -> P_target):
    A(start, target) = min over all neurons n_i of the gain change
    required to move from P_start to P_target via single-node modulation.
    If no single-node change suffices, A = infinity.

  For multi-node transitions, search over pairs: for each pair (n_i, n_j),
  compute the minimum total gain change |delta_g_i| + |delta_g_j| required
  to reach P_target from P_start.

  EVOLUTIONARY DISTANCE between states:
    D(start, target) = shortest path in the transition graph weighted
    by accessibility scores.

Visualization:
  Network diagram:
    5 nodes (phenotypic states) arranged in a circle or force-directed layout.
    Edge thickness proportional to 1/accessibility (thick = easy transition).
    Edge color: green = achievable by single-node inhibitory change,
    orange = achievable by single-node excitatory change,
    red = requires multi-node change, gray = not achievable.

  Edge labels: the specific neuron(s) whose gain change mediates each
  transition (e.g., "mAL_m1, delta_g = 0.5").

  Node size proportional to the "basin of attraction" -- the fraction of
  gain parameter space that maps to each phenotypic state.

  Inset: Heatmap of pairwise evolutionary distances D(P_i, P_j).

Expected results:
  - P1 (selective) <-> P2 (moderate) <-> P3 (promiscuous): These
    transitions should be EASY (green edges, low accessibility scores),
    mediated by single-node changes in mAL subtypes. This is exactly
    the variation observed across the 16 Ryba strains.

  - P3 (promiscuous) -> P4 (reversed): This should be HARD (red edge,
    high accessibility, requires multi-node changes). Reversed preference
    is not observed in natural populations, and the circuit architecture
    explains why -- it would require coordinated changes in multiple
    pathways.

  - Any state -> P5 (courtship-off): This should be moderately easy
    via excitatory gain reduction (orange edges), explaining why courtship
    can be abolished by many different mutations but preference can only
    be shifted by specific ones.

  - P1 (selective) has a large basin of attraction (robust state),
    consistent with selectivity being the ancestral state that is
    maintained by stabilizing selection.

Connection to Ryba et al.:
  Ryba et al. document within-species variation along the P1-P2-P3 axis
  but no evidence of reversed preference (P4) or courtship abolition (P5).
  The accessibility network provides a circuit-level explanation: the
  observed variation falls along the "easy" transitions, and the
  unobserved variation falls along the "hard" transitions. Natural
  selection operates within the circuit's accessibility constraints.

III.  MATHEMATICAL FRAMEWORK
================================================================================

1. GAIN-MODIFIED PROPAGATION

Let W be the signed adjacency matrix (N x N), s0 the input stimulus vector,
and g = (g_1, ..., g_N) the gain vector (g_i = 1 for all neurons at baseline).

Define the diagonal gain matrix G = diag(g).

The gain-modified adjacency matrix is:
  W_g = G . W

i.e., W_g[i, j] = g_i * W[i, j].

Signal propagation with sustained input:
  x(0) = s0
  x(t+1) = f(W_g^T . x(t) + s0)

where f is the sigmoid_rectified activation: f(z) = max(0, tanh(beta * z)).

After T steps, the final activation is x(T; g, s0).

2. PREFERENCE INDEX

  PI(g) = [x(T; g, S_con)_target - x(T; g, S_het)_target] /
          [x(T; g, S_con)_target + x(T; g, S_het)_target]

where the subscript "target" denotes the mean activation across target neurons.

3. LEVERAGE

The leverage of neuron i with respect to target group k is:
  L_k(i) = |partial PI_k / partial g_i| evaluated at g = 1

Computed numerically:
  L_k(i) ~ |PI_k(g_i = 1 + epsilon) - PI_k(g_i = 1 - epsilon)| / (2*epsilon)

with epsilon = 0.01.

4. FRAGILITY

For neuron i, define the useful range [g_min, g_max] such that:
  (a) VIGOR(g) = x(T; g, S_con)_target + x(T; g, S_het)_target > v_threshold
      where v_threshold = 0.5 * VIGOR(g=1)
  (b) There exists g' in [g, g + delta] such that PI(g') != PI(g)
      (preference is not frozen)

  F(i) = 1 - (g_max - g_min) / g_total_range

5. EVOLUTIONARY ACCESSIBILITY

For transition from phenotypic state P_a to P_b:
  A(a -> b) = min_{i} (|delta_g_i|) such that:
    - At g_i = 1 + delta_g_i (with all other gains at 1), the system is in state P_b
    - The system passes through only "functional" states along the path
      from g_i = 1 to g_i = 1 + delta_g_i (no courtship abolition)

For multi-node transitions:
  A_multi(a -> b) = min_{(i,j)} (|delta_g_i| + |delta_g_j|)

Evolutionary distance:
  D(a, b) = shortest weighted path in the transition graph with weights A.

  class GainModulator:
      """Modifies the adjacency matrix by applying gain factors."""

      def __init__(self, W_signed, type_names):
          self.W = W_signed  # baseline signed matrix
          self.type_to_idx = dict(zip(type_names["type"], type_names["index"]))
          self.idx_to_type = dict(zip(type_names["index"], type_names["type"]))
          self.n = W_signed.shape[0]

      def apply_gain(self, gain_dict):
          """Apply gain factors to specified neurons.
          gain_dict: {type_name: gain_value, ...}
          Returns modified sparse matrix.
          """
          gains = np.ones(self.n)
          for type_name, gain_val in gain_dict.items():
              if type_name in self.type_to_idx:
                  gains[self.type_to_idx[type_name]] = gain_val
          G = sparse.diags(gains)
          return G.dot(self.W)

      def sweep_single_gain(self, neuron_type, gain_range, stimulus_dict,
                             target_indices, n_steps=3, sustained=True):
          """Sweep gain at one neuron, compute PI and vigor at each value."""
          results = []
          for g in gain_range:
              W_mod = self.apply_gain({neuron_type: g})
              propagator = SignalPropagator(W_mod, "sigmoid_rectified",
                                            {"beta": 5.0})
              activations = {}
              for stim_name, s0 in stimulus_dict.items():
                  x = propagator.propagate(s0, n_steps, sustained=sustained)
                  activations[stim_name] = np.mean([x[i] for i in target_indices])
              results.append({"gain": g, **activations})
          return pd.DataFrame(results)

================================================================================
V.  CONNECTIONS TO RYBA ET AL. EXPERIMENTAL FINDINGS
================================================================================

This figure makes several specific, testable predictions based on the
connectome that align with or extend Ryba et al.'s experimental findings:

1. CONVERGENT IDENTIFICATION OF mAL AS KEY LOCUS
   Ryba et al. identify mAL through a forward genetic screen combined with
   calcium imaging. Our leverage analysis (Panel A) identifies the same
   neurons through a purely computational approach -- screening all ~500
   neurons in the courtship circuit for their influence on preference index.
   This convergence validates both approaches and suggests that connectome-
   based leverage analysis could identify gain modulation sites in circuits
   where experimental screens are not feasible.

2. SELECTIVITY SWITCH PHENOTYPE
   Ryba et al. show that mAL depolarization makes flies selective while
   mAL hyperpolarization makes them promiscuous, with bidirectional
   effects that are quantitatively consistent. Our gain sweep (Panel B)
   predicts exactly this: a monotonic, steep relationship between mAL gain
   and preference index. The quantitative shape of this curve (sigmoid,
   with the transition centered near baseline) is a prediction that could
   be tested by parametric optogenetic stimulation of mAL.

3. ONE-DIMENSIONAL BEHAVIORAL VARIATION
   Ryba et al. show that 16 strains form a continuous 1D gradient from
   selective to promiscuous. Our landscape analysis (Panels C-D) predicts
   that this 1D gradient maps to a 1D manifold in gain space (variation
   along the mAL axis), with no systematic variation along other axes.
   The R^2 of strain positions projected onto the mAL axis should be
   > 0.9.

4. WHY mAL AND NOT OTHER NEURONS
   Ryba et al. note that "multigenic architecture may nevertheless belie
   a relatively simple neural logic" but do not fully explain WHY the
   circuit converges on this simple logic. Our fragility analysis
   (Panel E) provides the answer: mAL neurons are uniquely positioned
   in the circuit as "ideal evolutionary knobs" -- high leverage, high
   robustness, and high efficiency. Other high-leverage neurons are
   fragile (small gain changes break courtship) or inefficient (large
   gain changes produce small selectivity shifts).

5. E/I ASYMMETRY PRINCIPLE
   Ryba et al. show that the key mechanism is GABAergic inhibition from
   mAL to P1. Our E/I asymmetry analysis (Panel F) generalizes this to
   a circuit-theoretic principle: inhibitory gain modulation preferentially
   affects stimulus DISCRIMINATION, while excitatory gain modulation
   preferentially affects response MAGNITUDE. This predicts that in other
   circuits where stimulus preference varies across populations (e.g.,
   olfactory food preference, visual pattern preference), the substrate
   of variation should also be found in inhibitory interneurons.

6. EVOLUTIONARY ACCESSIBILITY CONSTRAINTS
   Ryba et al. observe within-species variation along the selective-
   promiscuous axis but not reversed preference. Our accessibility
   analysis (Panel G) explains this: the selective-to-promiscuous
   transition is "easy" (single-node mAL gain changes), while preference
   reversal would require coordinated multi-node changes that are
   evolutionarily unlikely within a species.


================================================================================
VI.  BIOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE
================================================================================

This figure establishes three conceptual contributions:

1. GAIN LANDSCAPES AS A TOOL FOR EVOLUTIONARY NEUROSCIENCE
   The concept of a "gain landscape" -- the mapping from synaptic efficacy
   parameters to behavioral phenotype -- provides a quantitative framework
   for understanding how circuit architecture constrains behavioral
   evolution. Just as fitness landscapes map genotype to fitness, gain
   landscapes map neural parameters to behavioral phenotype. The topology
   of this landscape (ridges, valleys, basins) determines which
   evolutionary paths are accessible and which are blocked.

2. THE CONNECTOME PREDICTS EVOLUTIONARY SUBSTRATES
   By computing leverage, fragility, and accessibility from connectome
   data alone (without any experimental manipulation), we can predict
   which circuit nodes are the most likely substrates of evolutionary
   variation. The validation against Ryba et al.'s experimental findings
   demonstrates that this approach works -- the connectome contains
   sufficient information to identify "evolutionary hotspots" in neural
   circuits.

3. THE E/I ASYMMETRY PRINCIPLE
   The finding that inhibitory gain modulation selectively affects stimulus
   preference while excitatory gain modulation affects response magnitude
   is a general principle that should apply to any circuit performing
   stimulus discrimination. This connects the specific biology of
   Drosophila mate preference to broader questions about how neural
   circuits evolve to change their computational properties.

IX.  ADDITIONAL TECHNICAL NOTES
================================================================================

1. STIMULUS PATTERN DEFINITIONS

The choice of S_con and S_het matters quantitatively. We propose:

  S_con (conspecific female stimulus):
    ppk23 = 1, ppk25 = 1, DA1 = 1, VA1d = 1, auditory = 0, visual = 1
    Rationale: Female D. melanogaster produce cVA (DA1), have specific
    cuticular hydrocarbons detected by ppk23/ppk25, and visual cues.
    No auditory because the female does not sing. VA1d responds to
    cis-vaccenyl acetate (in some contexts).

  S_het (heterospecific / male-like stimulus):
    ppk23 = 1, VA1v = 1, auditory = 0, visual = 1, ppk25 = 0
    Rationale: Male D. melanogaster produce 7-T (VA1v), have male
    CHCs detected by ppk23, and visual cues. ppk25 may respond less
    to male CHCs (per Ryba et al., the key differential is 7-T on VA1v).

  These can be refined based on the specific experimental protocols in
  Ryba et al. The qualitative results should be robust to reasonable
  variations in stimulus definitions, as the key leverage differences
  come from circuit topology, not stimulus details.

2. NORMALIZATION CONSIDERATIONS

All computations use the post-normalized (column-normalized) adjacency
matrix, which represents the fraction of each neuron's total input coming
from each presynaptic partner. This is the most biologically appropriate
normalization for gain modulation analysis because:
  - It preserves the relative importance of different inputs
  - Gain changes at a presynaptic neuron affect its contribution to
    all postsynaptic partners proportionally
  - The total input to each neuron sums to ~1 at baseline

Results should be checked for robustness against pre-normalization and
raw counts, as in the existing sensitivity analysis (run_analysis.py
sensitivity_params).

3. POTENTIAL LIMITATIONS

  (a) The model uses a static nonlinearity (sigmoid_rectified) that may
      not capture the full dynamics of real neural gain modulation. In
      particular, synaptic plasticity, neuromodulation, and homeostatic
      compensation are not modeled. The gain landscape should therefore
      be interpreted as describing the INSTANTANEOUS effect of gain
      changes, not the long-term evolutionary equilibrium.

  (b) The connectome provides synaptic count-based weights but not
      functional synaptic strength. Gain variation in Ryba et al. may
      reflect changes in synaptic strength, neurotransmitter release
      probability, or receptor density, all of which are collapsed into
      a single gain parameter in our model.

  (c) The stimulus patterns S_con and S_het are idealized binary vectors.
      Real pheromone stimuli produce graded responses with complex
      temporal dynamics. The preference index PI is therefore a
      steady-state approximation.

  (d) We model gain changes at the level of neuron TYPES (all neurons of
      a type share the same gain). In reality, individual neurons within
      a type may vary. This is acceptable for the present analysis because
      Ryba et al. show that strain differences affect entire neuron
      populations (all mAL neurons), not individual cells.

4. VALIDATION OPPORTUNITIES

  (a) QUANTITATIVE FIT: If per-strain mAL calcium imaging data from
      Ryba et al. becomes available, the predicted g_mAL values (Panel D)
      can be compared to measured mAL response amplitudes. The predicted
      relationship is monotonic and roughly linear.

  (b) PERTURBATION PREDICTIONS: The gain sweep (Panel B) predicts the
      quantitative effect of parametric optogenetic stimulation of mAL.
      Specifically, the PI curve predicts that intermediate levels of mAL
      activation should produce intermediate levels of selectivity, not
      a binary switch. This could be tested with graded optogenetic
      protocols.

  (c) NOVEL TARGETS: If the leverage analysis (Panel A) identifies
      high-leverage neurons BEYOND the mAL cluster, these are predictions
      for new experimental targets. Neurons with high leverage but high
      fragility would be predicted to affect courtship when manipulated
      but would not be expected to show natural variation (because
      variation at these sites would be deleterious).
