Figure 5: Temporal Dynamics of Courtship Decision-Making
=========================================================
A connectome-grounded model of moment-to-moment sensory evaluation
through transient inhibitory gating

Research Design Document
========================

Authors' note: This document specifies a NEW figure (Fig 5) for the
pheromone/courtship paper.  Fig 5 focuses on TIME -- how the courtship
circuit implements sequential sensory sampling and decision switching --
whereas Fig 6 (the existing figure) focuses on STATIC integration, i.e.,
which modalities converge on which targets and with what interaction
profiles.  Together, Figs 5 and 6 form a complementary pair: Fig 5 asks
"When does information arrive, and how does the circuit respond to
sequential inputs?"; Fig 6 asks "What is computed when all information
has arrived?"


========================================================================
1.  FIGURE NARRATIVE: HOW THE PANELS TELL A SEQUENTIAL STORY
========================================================================

The narrative arc of Fig 5 is built on a single organizing principle:
TIME.  Courtship is not a one-shot classification.  A male Drosophila
approaches a potential mate, contacts her cuticle, evaluates her
pheromone profile, and -- if she is heterospecific or unreceptive --
pauses pursuit and reorients to sample a new target.  Ryba et al. (2025)
demonstrated that this cycle is mediated by transient inhibitory gating
of P1 neurons through mAL interneurons, driven by contact chemosensory
cues (ppk23/ppk25).  The male does not become permanently disinterested;
he becomes momentarily "blind" to the current pursuit target while his
arousal system remains active.  The duration and depth of that inhibitory
transient determines whether he switches targets.

Fig 5 unpacks this temporal decision process using the male CNS
connectome and the signal propagation framework already developed for
Fig 6.  The story proceeds in three acts:

ACT 1 -- THE MECHANISM (Panels A-C)
  "Here is the decision cycle, here is what transient inhibition looks
   like in the connectome's temporal dynamics, and here is how fast
   different signals arrive."

  Panel A introduces the state-transition model: the cycle of approach,
  sense, evaluate, and continue-or-switch, annotated with the connectome
  pathways that implement each transition.

  Panel B shows WHAT HAPPENS in time when a male encounters sequential
  sensory events.  Using propagate_trajectory(), we simulate a scenario
  where sustained conspecific pheromone input (DA1+VA1v) is interrupted
  by a transient contact chemosensory pulse (ppk23) and then resumes.
  The result is a time-series at aSP-f/aSP-g/mAL-type neurons showing
  the characteristic DIP in excitatory drive during the inhibitory
  transient, followed by recovery.

  Panel C quantifies the "integration time constants" -- how many
  propagation steps it takes for each sensory channel to reach each
  target group via excitatory versus inhibitory paths.  This reveals the
  temporal ordering of information arrival.

ACT 2 -- THE DECISION SPACE (Panels D-E)
  "Given these dynamics, how does the circuit make a binary decision
   (continue vs. switch), and what determines the outcome?"

  Panel D maps the decision as a 2D phase diagram: excitatory drive
  (x-axis) vs. inhibitory drive (y-axis), with color encoding switching
  probability.  Experimental conditions from Ryba et al. -- conspecific,
  heterospecific-selective, heterospecific-promiscuous -- are plotted as
  points in this space.

  Panel E formalizes the decision as a drift-diffusion process.
  Excitatory connectome paths provide positive drift; inhibitory paths
  provide negative drift.  When cumulative evidence at P1-like neurons
  drops below a threshold, courtship pauses.  This framework predicts
  decision time, error rate, and switching probability as functions of
  the connectome-derived drift rates.

ACT 3 -- THE PREDICTIONS (Panels F-G)
  "What does the temporal architecture of the connectome predict about
   real behavior?"

  Panel F visualizes the "signal race" -- a timeline showing when
  visual, olfactory, and contact chemosensory information arrives at
  decision neurons.  The temporal ordering constrains the decision
  process: fast-but-nonspecific signals (visual) arrive before
  slow-but-specific ones (contact chemosensory), creating a window
  during which the male pursues ANY moving target before species-specific
  information arrives to confirm or abort.

  Panel G simulates Ryba et al.'s closed-loop paradigm: a male
  receiving repeated inhibitory pulses as he approaches a "fictive
  heterospecific."  The connectome propagation predicts how quickly
  preference develops as a function of the inhibitory/excitatory time
  constant ratio.

PANEL B: Temporal Activation Profiles Under Alternating Stimuli
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  This is the core computational panel.  We extend the existing
  propagate_trajectory() method to simulate SEQUENTIAL stimulus events.

  Simulation protocol:
    1. Load the signed adjacency matrix (post-normalized, as in Fig 6).
    2. Define three stimulus epochs:
       - Epoch 1 (steps 0-4):  Sustained conspecific pheromone.
         s0 = encode(DA1) + encode(VA1v).  Sustained mode.
       - Epoch 2 (steps 5-7):  Transient contact chemosensory pulse
         ADDED to the ongoing pheromone stimulus.
         s0 = encode(DA1) + encode(VA1v) + encode(ppk23).  Pulse mode
         for ppk23 (injected at step 5 only); sustained for DA1+VA1v.
       - Epoch 3 (steps 8-12): Return to conspecific pheromone only.
         s0 = encode(DA1) + encode(VA1v).  Sustained mode.

    3. Implementing multi-epoch simulation:
       The existing propagate_trajectory() handles a single stimulus
       vector for all steps.  For Fig 5B, we need to extend this to
       allow the input vector to change across steps.  The proposed
       implementation:

       def propagate_multi_epoch(self, epochs, n_steps_per_epoch):
           """
           epochs: list of (s0, sustained_bool) tuples
           n_steps_per_epoch: list of int, one per epoch
           """
           x = np.zeros(self.n)
           trajectory = [x.copy()]
           for (s0, sustained), n_steps in zip(epochs, n_steps_per_epoch):
               for t in range(n_steps):
                   x = self.Wt.dot(x)
                   if sustained:
                       x = x + s0
                   else:
                       if t == 0:
                           x = x + s0  # inject pulse at start of epoch only
                   x = self.activation_fn(x, **self.activation_params)
                   trajectory.append(x.copy())
           return np.array(trajectory)

       A cleaner alternative that keeps the existing API intact:
       concatenate trajectories step by step, using the final state
       of one epoch as the initial state of the next, and alternating
       which input vector is clamped.

    4. Read out activations at:
       - aSP-f neurons (aspf target group: LH001m, LH002m, LH003m,
         LH006m, LH008m, AVLP753m, PVLP205m, SLP160)
       - aSP-g neurons (aspg target group: aSP-g3Am, AVLP700m,
         AVLP704m, AVLP727m, AVLP750m)
       - mAL-type neurons from vAB3_downstream: mAL_m1, mAL_m3c,
         mAL_m8, mAL_m9, mAL_m3b, mAL_m2a (these are the GABAergic
         intermediaries)
       - P1 neurons (not in target groups but present in the type_names
         and adjacency matrix; can be read out by encoding P1 subtypes)

    5. Run three conditions and overlay:
       (a) Wild-type: DA1+VA1v sustained + ppk23 transient pulse
       (b) ppk23-silenced: DA1+VA1v sustained only (no ppk23 pulse)
       (c) mAL-enhanced: DA1+VA1v sustained + ppk23 transient with
           2x scaling on ppk23 input vector

Visualization:
  - Multi-panel time-series plot.
  - X-axis: propagation step (= abstract time).
  - Y-axis: mean activation within each neuron group.
  - Separate subplot rows for:
    Row 1: aSP-f mean activation (excitatory command, "P1-like")
    Row 2: aSP-g mean activation
    Row 3: mAL-type mean activation (inhibitory intermediary)
  - Three line colors per subplot for the three conditions (WT, ppk23-
    silenced, mAL-enhanced).
  - Vertical shaded band marking the ppk23 pulse epoch.
  - Annotations:
    - Arrow pointing to the DIP in aSP-f/aSP-g activation during the
      ppk23 epoch ("transient inhibitory gating").
    - Arrow pointing to the LACK of dip in the ppk23-silenced condition.
    - Arrow pointing to the DEEPER dip in the mAL-enhanced condition.

Expected results:
  - aSP-f and aSP-g should show a clear decrease in activation during
    the ppk23 pulse epoch (because ppk23 routes through GABAergic mAL
    neurons that inhibit P1/aSP-f/aSP-g targets -- as already shown in
    Fig 6's interaction analysis where ppk23 often produces OPPOSING
    interactions with olfactory channels at aSP-f/aSP-g targets).
  - mAL neurons should show a SPIKE during the ppk23 epoch.
  - After the pulse, aSP-f/aSP-g should recover toward the pre-pulse
    sustained pheromone baseline.
  - The depth and duration of the dip encode the "switching signal."

Technical notes:
  - Nonlinearity: use sigmoid_rectified (beta=5.0) to match the
    default analysis in Fig 6.
  - Normalization: post-synaptic (column-normalized), consistent with
    Fig 6.
  - Number of propagation steps: 3-5 per epoch, giving 9-15 total steps.
    The existing framework uses 3 steps for steady-state analysis; for
    temporal dynamics we use more to see transient behavior.
  - Use leaky_relu (alpha=0.1) as a sensitivity check, since it
    preserves inhibitory signal propagation better than ReLU.

  Implementation file: a new Python script,
    combinatorial_signal_flow/fig5_temporal_dynamics.py
  Plot output: fig5/plots/panel_B/

PANEL C: Integration Time Constants
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  For each of the 7 sensory channels, propagate a pulse input and
  record the activation trajectory at each target group.  Compute the
  "effective arrival time" as the propagation step at which activation
  at the target first exceeds a threshold (e.g., 10% of peak).

  Specifically:
    1. For each channel ch in {DA1, VA1v, VA1d, auditory, visual,
       ppk23, ppk25}:
       a. Encode ch as input vector s0.
       b. Run propagate_trajectory(s0, n_steps=6, sustained=False).
          (Pulse mode: single injection, no re-clamping.)
       c. For each target group tg in {aspf, aspg, vAB3_downstream,
          PPN1_downstream}:
          - Compute mean activation across target neurons at each step.
          - Find t_arrival: first step where mean activation > 0.1 * max.
          - Find t_peak: step of maximum activation.
          - Find t_half: step where activation first drops below 50% of
            peak (decay time).

    2. Separately categorize paths as excitatory or inhibitory using
       the sign information from the adjacency matrix.  The approach:
       - Run propagation on the UNSIGNED matrix to get total signal
         arrival time.
       - Run propagation on a matrix where only excitatory weights are
         kept (zero out inhibitory rows) to get excitatory arrival time.
       - Run propagation on a matrix where only inhibitory weights are
         kept to get inhibitory arrival time.
       - Compare: does inhibition arrive BEFORE or AFTER excitation?

Visualization:
  - Dot-and-whisker plot or "arrival time distribution" plot.
  - X-axis: sensory channel (ordered by modality group: olfactory,
    auditory, visual, contact).
  - Y-axis: propagation step of arrival.
  - Two series per channel: excitatory arrival (filled circles) and
    inhibitory arrival (open circles).
  - Faceted by target group (aspf, aspg, vAB3_downstream,
    PPN1_downstream).
  - Key visual element: draw a horizontal band at each target group's
    "decision window" -- the interval between excitatory and inhibitory
    arrival.

  Alternative visualization (if the dot plot is too sparse given the
  discrete step counts):
  - Heatmap with channels as rows, steps as columns, color = activation
    level.  Separate heatmaps for excitatory and inhibitory components.

Expected results:
  - Olfactory channels (DA1, VA1v, VA1d) should arrive relatively fast
    at aSP-f/aSP-g (2 hops: ORN -> PN -> target), consistent with the
    path analysis in Fig 6.
  - Contact chemosensory (ppk23, ppk25) may arrive at aSP-f/aSP-g via
    longer paths (ppk23 -> LgLG/WG -> mAL -> aSP-f/aSP-g), but the
    inhibitory component may be FASTER than the excitatory component
    because the GABAergic mAL neurons are positioned as 2nd-order relays
    in the contact chemosensory pathway.
  - Visual signal should be fast but non-specific (reaches both target
    groups similarly).
  - KEY PREDICTION: if inhibitory paths from ppk23 are faster or equal
    to excitatory paths from ppk23, the circuit implements a
    "guilty until proven innocent" strategy -- initial contact triggers
    suppression, and only if the pheromone profile matches conspecific
    does the excitatory drive recover to override inhibition.

Technical notes:
  - This analysis uses the path data already computed for Fig 6
    (paths.all, which includes path lengths and valence information)
    AND the propagation framework.
  - For the excitatory-only / inhibitory-only matrices: create
    modified versions of the signed adjacency matrix by zeroing out
    the appropriate rows.
  - This extends the layer-by-layer analysis in Panel K of Fig 6 but
    focuses on temporal ordering rather than convergence per se.

  Implementation: fig5_temporal_dynamics.py (same script as Panel B)
  Plot output: fig5/plots/panel_C/

PANEL D: Switching Probability Phase Diagram
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  Model the courtship switching decision as a function of two competing
  inputs:
    - Excitatory drive E: activation at aSP-f/aSP-g from olfactory
      channels (DA1 + VA1v), obtained via propagation.
    - Inhibitory drive I: activation at mAL neurons from contact
      channels (ppk23), obtained via propagation.

  The switching probability is modeled as:

    P(switch) = sigma(alpha * (I - beta * E))

  where sigma is a logistic function, alpha controls the steepness,
  and beta sets the excitation/inhibition balance.

  To build the phase diagram:
    1. Compute the baseline excitatory activation E_baseline at aSP-f
       from DA1+VA1v (sustained, 3 steps).
    2. Compute the baseline inhibitory activation I_baseline at mAL
       neurons from ppk23 (pulse, 3 steps).
    3. Create a 2D grid:
       E_range = np.linspace(0, 2*E_baseline, 50)
       I_range = np.linspace(0, 2*I_baseline, 50)
    4. For each (E, I) pair, compute P(switch).
    5. Map experimental conditions:
       - Conspecific female: E = E_baseline (high), I = low (ppk23
         activation minimal for conspecific).
       - Heterospecific (selective male): E = 0.5*E_baseline (reduced
         olfactory match), I = I_baseline (full ppk23 activation for
         heterospecific contact pheromones).
       - Heterospecific (promiscuous male): E = 0.5*E_baseline,
         I = 0.5*I_baseline (reduced inhibitory response, consistent
         with Ryba et al.'s finding that promiscuous strains have
         weaker mAL-mediated inhibition).

  Alternative, more biophysically grounded approach:
    Instead of a logistic function, use the actual propagation dynamics.
    For each (E_scale, I_scale) pair:
    - Run multi-epoch simulation with:
      Epoch 1: s0 = E_scale * (DA1 + VA1v), sustained
      Epoch 2: s0 = E_scale * (DA1 + VA1v) + I_scale * ppk23, transient
    - Measure the depth of the activation dip at aSP-f relative to
      baseline.
    - Define P(switch) = probability that the dip exceeds a threshold
      (set as fraction of baseline activation).

Visualization:
  - 2D heatmap / phase diagram.
  - X-axis: excitatory drive (E), labeled "Olfactory pheromone drive"
  - Y-axis: inhibitory drive (I), labeled "Contact chemosensory drive"
  - Color: P(switch) on a diverging blue-white-red scale (blue = low
    switching, red = high switching).
  - Overlaid points for the three experimental conditions with labels.
  - Diagonal line marking the decision boundary (P(switch) = 0.5).
  - Optional contour lines at P = 0.2, 0.5, 0.8.

Expected results:
  - The decision boundary should be a roughly diagonal line from high-E/
    low-I (continue courtship) to low-E/high-I (switch).
  - Conspecific female maps to the "continue" region.
  - Heterospecific with selective male maps to the "switch" region.
  - Heterospecific with promiscuous male maps near the boundary
    (intermediate switching probability), consistent with the variable
    behavior Ryba et al. observed.
  - The slope of the boundary encodes the circuit's excitation/inhibition
    balance, which is a property of the connectome wiring (number and
    weight of excitatory vs. inhibitory paths).

  Implementation: fig5_temporal_dynamics.py
  Plot output: fig5/plots/panel_D/

PANEL E: Drift-Diffusion Model
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  Frame the courtship decision as a drift-diffusion process (DDM),
  which is the standard computational framework for binary decisions
  in neuroscience.

  The DDM for courtship:
    dx/dt = mu + sigma * dW(t)

  where:
    x(t) = "evidence accumulator" at P1-like neurons
    mu   = drift rate = f(excitatory drive) - g(inhibitory drive)
    sigma = noise amplitude
    dW(t) = Wiener process (noise)
    Decision: when x(t) < threshold_low -> SWITCH
              when x(t) > threshold_high -> CONTINUE

  Connectome-derived drift rates:
    mu_E = sum of signed path strengths from DA1+VA1v to aSP-f
    mu_I = sum of signed path strengths from ppk23 to aSP-f via mAL
    mu = mu_E - mu_I

  For each "encounter type" (conspecific, heterospecific-selective,
  heterospecific-promiscuous), set different mu values based on the
  relative activation strength of excitatory vs. inhibitory pathways.

  Compute analytically (or via Monte Carlo simulation):
    - Mean decision time: T = (threshold_high - threshold_low) / mu
      (for the absorbing boundary DDM)
    - Error rate: P(error) = 1 / (1 + exp(2 * mu * threshold / sigma^2))
    - Switching probability: P(switch) from the DDM

  Monte Carlo approach (more flexible):
    1. For each condition (conspecific, heterospecific-selective,
       heterospecific-promiscuous):
       a. Set mu from connectome-derived path strengths.
       b. Simulate 10,000 DDM trials.
       c. Record decision time and outcome (switch vs. continue).
    2. Compare distributions across conditions.

Visualization:
  Three sub-panels:
    E1: Sample DDM trajectories.
      - X-axis: time (propagation steps).
      - Y-axis: accumulated evidence x(t).
      - Show 10-20 sample trajectories for each condition, colored by
        condition (conspecific = green, heterospecific-selective = red,
        heterospecific-promiscuous = orange).
      - Horizontal lines for decision thresholds.
      - Annotations: "CONTINUE" at upper threshold, "SWITCH" at lower.

    E2: Decision time distributions.
      - Violin plots or histograms of decision times by condition.
      - Prediction: conspecific -> fast CONTINUE; heterospecific-
        selective -> fast SWITCH; promiscuous -> slow, variable.

    E3: Switching probability vs. drift rate.
      - X-axis: drift rate mu (from strongly excitatory to strongly
        inhibitory).
      - Y-axis: P(switch).
      - Sigmoidal curve with three conditions marked as points.

Expected results:
  - Conspecific encounter: positive drift (excitatory > inhibitory) ->
    fast approach, low switching probability.
  - Heterospecific (selective male): negative drift -> fast switching.
  - Heterospecific (promiscuous male): drift near zero -> slow decisions,
    high variability, intermediate switching.
  - The DDM framework explains Ryba et al.'s observation that promiscuous
    males take LONGER to develop preferences -- they are operating near
    the decision boundary where noise dominates.

  Implementation: fig5_temporal_dynamics.py
  Plot output: fig5/plots/panel_E/

PANEL F: Signal Race Between Modalities
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  This panel uses the propagate_trajectory() function in pulse mode to
  visualize the temporal ordering of information arrival from different
  modality groups at the decision neurons.

  Protocol:
    1. For each modality group:
       - Olfactory: encode(DA1) + encode(VA1v) + encode(VA1d)
       - Auditory: encode(auditory)
       - Visual: encode(visual)
       - Contact: encode(ppk23) + encode(ppk25)
    2. Propagate each group's input separately (pulse mode, 6 steps).
    3. Read out activation at aSP-f and aSP-g at each step.
    4. For each group, record:
       - Arrival time (first step with activation > threshold)
       - Peak time
       - Peak amplitude
       - Species specificity index (defined below)

  Species specificity:
    Based on the biological literature, assign a "species-specificity
    score" to each modality group:
    - Visual: LOW (conspecific and heterospecific females look similar)
    - Auditory: MEDIUM (courtship song is species-specific but
      production varies)
    - Olfactory: HIGH (pheromone blends are species-specific)
    - Contact chemosensory: HIGHEST (cuticular hydrocarbons are the
      most species-specific cue, and ppk23 mediates the rejection
      response to heterospecific CHCs)

Visualization:
  Timeline / Gantt-chart style:
  - X-axis: propagation step (time).
  - Y-axis: modality group (visual, auditory, olfactory, contact).
  - For each modality: horizontal bar from arrival time to decay time,
    with width proportional to activation amplitude.
  - Color: species-specificity (gradient from light/cool for low
    specificity to dark/warm for high specificity).
  - Overlay at the top: a "decision certainty" curve that increases as
    each modality's signal arrives -- starts low when only visual is
    available, increases when olfactory arrives, and reaches maximum
    when contact chemosensory information is integrated.

  Alternative: stacked area chart.
  - X-axis: propagation step.
  - Y-axis: cumulative activation at aSP-f (stacked by modality group).
  - Shows how the total evidence builds over time as each modality
    contributes.

Expected results:
  - Visual signal arrives first (LC10 neurons have direct projections
    to AVLP, close to aSP-f/aSP-g).
  - Olfactory signal arrives at step 2 (ORN -> PN -> aSP-f, a canonical
    2-hop path).
  - Contact chemosensory signal arrives at step 2-3 (ppk23 -> LgLG ->
    mAL -> aSP-f, a 3-hop path, but some 2-hop paths may exist via
    direct projections to AVLP neurons in the target set).
  - KEY INSIGHT: the ordering is visual (fast, nonspecific) -> olfactory
    (medium, specific) -> contact (slow, most specific).  This creates
    a temporal hierarchy where the male's initial pursuit is driven by
    nonspecific visual cues, then refined by olfactory identity, and
    finally gated by contact chemosensory evaluation.  The circuit
    implements a "coarse-to-fine" sensory evaluation strategy.

  Plot output: fig5/plots/panel_F/

PANEL G: Preference Learning in Closed-Loop Simulation
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Data/computation:
  Simulate Ryba et al.'s closed-loop optogenetic paradigm using the
  connectome propagation framework.

  Experimental paradigm (Ryba et al.):
    - Male in arena with two fictive "targets" (LEDs).
    - When male approaches target A, he receives optogenetic activation
      of mAL neurons (simulating heterospecific contact cue).
    - When male approaches target B, no inhibitory input.
    - Over trials, the male develops a preference for target B.
    - After ~80% of switches away from A, stable preference emerges.

  Connectome simulation:
    1. Define a "trial" as one approach-contact-evaluate cycle.
    2. For each trial n:
       a. If male is near target A:
          - Input: sustained pheromone (DA1+VA1v) + pulse ppk23
          - Propagate 3 steps.
          - Read aSP-f activation.  If dip > threshold -> switch to B.
       b. If male is near target B:
          - Input: sustained pheromone (DA1+VA1v) only
          - Propagate 3 steps.
          - aSP-f activation remains high -> continue at B.
    3. Track preference index over trials:
       PI(n) = (time at B - time at A) / (time at B + time at A)

    4. Model variations:
       (a) Vary the inhibitory strength (ppk23 scaling factor) to
           simulate selective vs. promiscuous males.
       (b) Vary the inhibitory/excitatory time constant ratio by
           changing the relative path lengths (use different propagation
           steps for inhibitory vs. excitatory components).
       (c) Add trial-to-trial noise (stochastic switching).

Visualization:
  - X-axis: trial number (1-50).
  - Y-axis: preference index PI(n), ranging from -1 (prefer A) to
    +1 (prefer B).
  - Multiple curves for different inhibitory strength levels:
    - Strong inhibition (selective male): rapid preference development
      (reaches PI > 0.8 by trial 10).
    - Moderate inhibition (promiscuous male): slow preference
      development (PI ~ 0.5 by trial 50).
    - Weak inhibition (very promiscuous): no preference development
      (PI ~ 0 throughout).
  - Inset: learning rate (slope of PI curve in early trials) vs.
    inhibitory/excitatory time constant ratio.

Expected results:
  - Preference development speed should scale with the strength of the
    inhibitory pathway, consistent with Ryba et al.'s finding that
    strain differences in mAL pathway strength predict courtship
    selectivity.
  - The ratio of inhibitory to excitatory time constants (from Panel C)
    should predict the learning rate: if inhibition arrives fast
    relative to excitation, switching is rapid and preference develops
    quickly; if inhibition is slow, the male "commits" to the current
    target before inhibition arrives, making switching rare.
  - Prediction: the speed of preference learning depends on two
    connectome parameters: (1) the ratio of inhibitory to excitatory
    path strengths (from the interaction analysis in Fig 6), and (2)
    the ratio of inhibitory to excitatory arrival times (from Panel C).

Fig 5 is designed to bridge between the structural analysis of the

    wait for slow contact chemosensory cues will be selective but slower

========================================================================
4.  DYNAMICAL SYSTEMS FRAMEWORK
========================================================================

The mathematical framework underlying Fig 5 extends the steady-state
propagation model used in Fig 6 into the temporal domain.

4.1  Discrete-time recurrence relation

The core equation in the existing framework is:

    x(t+1) = f(W^T x(t) + s(t))

where:
    x(t)    = N-dimensional activation vector at step t
    W       = signed adjacency matrix (N x N)
    s(t)    = sensory input vector (changes across epochs in Fig 5)
    f       = activation function (sigmoid_rectified with beta=5)

For pulse mode:  s(t) = s0 * delta(t, t_injection)
For sustained:   s(t) = s0  for all t in the epoch

4.2  Multi-epoch extension

Fig 5 introduces the concept of EPOCHS: contiguous time intervals
during which the input vector s(t) is constant.  Between epochs, s(t)
switches to a new configuration.  This models the sequential sensory
events in a courtship encounter:

    Epoch 1: s(t) = s_pheromone      (approach phase)
    Epoch 2: s(t) = s_pheromone + s_contact  (evaluation phase)
    Epoch 3: s(t) = s_pheromone      (recovery/decision phase)

The state vector x(t) evolves continuously across epoch boundaries --
there is no reset.  This means the history of prior stimulation
affects the response to subsequent inputs, creating a form of
short-term memory in the circuit.

4.3  Transient dynamics and time constants

The "effective time constant" of a pathway from channel ch to target
group tg is operationally defined as:

    tau_eff(ch, tg) = argmax_t [ x_tg(t) ]

where x_tg(t) is the mean activation at target group tg at step t
after a pulse injection from channel ch.  This gives the number of
propagation steps to peak activation, which corresponds to the
effective "synaptic delay" of the pathway.

For inhibitory pathways, the relevant quantity is the time to maximum
inhibition, measured as:

    tau_inh(ch, tg) = argmin_t [ x_tg(t) - x_tg_baseline(t) ]

where x_tg_baseline is the activation trajectory WITHOUT the
inhibitory input.

The CRITICAL QUANTITY for the decision process is the difference:

    Delta_tau(ch, tg) = tau_inh(ch, tg) - tau_exc(ch, tg)

If Delta_tau < 0 (inhibition arrives first): the circuit implements
  a precautionary strategy -- initial contact suppresses courtship,
  and only the sustained excitatory drive from conspecific pheromones
  can override the suppression.

If Delta_tau > 0 (excitation arrives first): the circuit implements
  a permissive strategy -- initial contact excites courtship, and
  inhibition can only RETROACTIVELY abort an ongoing approach.

If Delta_tau ~ 0 (simultaneous): the decision depends entirely on
  the RELATIVE STRENGTH of excitatory and inhibitory pathways,
  not their timing.

4.4  Connection to drift-diffusion model

The DDM (Panel E) is derived from the continuous-time limit of the
discrete propagation model.  If we define the decision variable as:

    z(t) = x_target(t) - x_target(baseline)

(deviation of target activation from resting state), then the
evolution of z(t) can be approximated as:

    z(t+1) - z(t) ~ mu + eta(t)

where mu is the mean drift (determined by the net signed path
strength from active channels to the target) and eta is noise
(arising from the stochastic nature of actual neural firing, not
modeled in the connectome propagation but added parametrically).

This connects the connectome analysis directly to the behavioral
decision:
    mu > 0: net excitatory drive -> continue courtship
    mu < 0: net inhibitory drive -> switch/abort
    |mu| small: slow, noisy decision -> variable behavior

4.5  Signed path analysis and effective drift

The drift rate mu for a given sensory context can be computed from
the signed adjacency matrix:

    mu(context) = sum_ch [ alpha(ch) * PathStrength(ch -> target) ]

where:
    alpha(ch) = stimulus intensity at channel ch
    PathStrength(ch -> target) = net signed path strength from ch to
      the target group, computed as the activation at the target after
      propagation of a unit input at ch.

This is exactly what the existing CombinatorialScreen computes for
the single-channel conditions.  The key insight is that the
INTERACTION SCORES (R_ij from Fig 6) represent the nonlinear
corrections to the drift rate when multiple channels are
co-activated.  A large positive R_ij (synergy) means that the
co-activation of channels i and j creates a LARGER drift than
expected from linear summation -- the circuit amplifies certain
channel combinations.  A large negative R_ij (opposition) means
the drift is REDUCED -- the circuit suppresses certain combinations.


========================================================================
5.  BIOLOGICAL SIGNIFICANCE
========================================================================

5.1  Why temporal dynamics matter for courtship

Courtship in Drosophila is a SEQUENTIAL process, not a snapshot
evaluation.  The male's decision unfolds over seconds to minutes,
during which he receives a continuous stream of multimodal sensory
information.  Different modalities provide information at different
rates:

  - Visual motion cues: available from a distance, updated at high
    frequency (visual tracking operates at >10 Hz).
  - Olfactory pheromones: available at intermediate distances, updated
    at moderate frequency (odor plume dynamics operate at ~1 Hz).
  - Contact chemosensory cues: available only during physical contact,
    providing high-resolution species identity information but requiring
    close approach.

This creates a natural temporal hierarchy: visual -> olfactory ->
contact.  The connectome must wire these inputs so that:
  (a) The male initiates pursuit based on fast, nonspecific cues (visual)
  (b) The male refines his assessment using medium-speed, species-specific
      cues (olfactory)
  (c) The male makes a final accept/reject decision based on slow,
      highly specific cues (contact chemosensory)

Fig 5 shows that the connectome architecture is consistent with this
temporal hierarchy: different sensory channels reach the decision
neurons at different speeds, and the inhibitory (abort) signal from
contact chemosensation arrives fast enough to gate the excitatory
(continue) signal.

5.2  Transient inhibition as a computational primitive

The mAL-mediated transient inhibition is not just a "stop signal" --
it is a computational primitive that enables the circuit to:

  (a) SAMPLE: by transiently suppressing pursuit, the male pauses long
      enough to reorient and encounter a new target.
  (b) COMPARE: the depth and duration of the inhibitory transient
      encode the "quality" of the current target (deeper dip = worse
      match), which can be compared across sequential encounters.
  (c) LEARN: repeated inhibitory transients from the same target build
      up a negative association, driving preference development.

This is analogous to the explore-exploit trade-off in reinforcement
learning: the inhibitory transient implements the "explore" phase by
interrupting the current "exploit" behavior (courtship pursuit).

5.3  Implications for behavioral evolution

Ryba et al.'s key finding is that strain variation in courtship
selectivity maps to variation in the mAL inhibitory pathway.  Our
connectome analysis suggests that this pathway is a particularly
effective "tuning knob" for the circuit because:

  (a) It is SPECIFIC: mAL neurons receive input from contact
      chemosensory channels (ppk23/ppk25) but not strongly from
      olfactory or visual channels.  Tuning mAL strength selectively
      affects the contact-to-decision pathway without disrupting
      visual pursuit or olfactory processing.

  (b) It is GATED: mAL activation depends on physical contact, so it
      only modulates the decision during the evaluation phase, not
      during approach or pursuit.

  (c) It is TRANSIENT: the inhibition decays naturally, ensuring that
      the male's arousal state (maintained by aSP-f/aSP-g and P1) is
      preserved across encounters.  This allows rapid resumption of
      courtship toward a new, potentially conspecific target.

  (d) It has the RIGHT time constant: our analysis predicts that mAL-
      mediated inhibition arrives fast enough to suppress the current
      pursuit before the male commits to copulation, but not so fast
      that it prevents any approach at all.

These properties make the mAL pathway an ideal substrate for natural
selection to tune courtship selectivity: small changes in synaptic
strength or receptor sensitivity at mAL neurons can shift the decision
boundary (Panel D) without disrupting the overall circuit architecture.

      decision process.

  (e) simulate_closed_loop(): Iterative simulation of the closed-loop
      preference learning paradigm.

  (f) Plotting functions for each panel (B through G).

6.2  Using propagate_trajectory()

The existing propagate_trajectory() method returns a (n_steps+1 x N)
matrix of activation values.  For Fig 5, we use this in two ways:

  (a) Single-channel pulse trajectories (Panel C, F):
      traj = propagator.propagate_trajectory(s0_ch, n_steps=6,
                                              sustained=False)
      For each target group, extract: traj[:, target_indices[tg]]

  (b) Multi-epoch trajectories (Panel B):
      Implemented as sequential calls to propagate(), carrying the
      state vector x across epoch boundaries:

      x = s0_epoch1.copy()
      full_traj = [x.copy()]
      for step in range(n_steps_epoch1):
          x = Wt.dot(x) + s0_epoch1  # sustained
          x = f(x)
          full_traj.append(x.copy())
      for step in range(n_steps_epoch2):
          raw = Wt.dot(x) + s0_epoch1  # pheromone continues
          if step == 0:
              raw += s0_ppk23  # contact pulse
          x = f(raw)
          full_traj.append(x.copy())
      # ... continue for epoch 3

6.3  Sustained vs. pulse mode semantics

In the existing framework:
  - Pulse (sustained=False): s0 injected at step 0 ONLY.
  - Sustained (sustained=True): s0 re-injected at EVERY step.

For Fig 5, we need a HYBRID mode where:
  - Pheromone channels are sustained (continuous exposure).
  - Contact chemosensory channels are pulse (brief touch).
  - Visual channels are sustained (continuous line-of-sight).

This requires the multi-epoch extension or a modified propagator that
accepts separate sustained and pulse input vectors:

    x(t+1) = f(W^T x(t) + s_sustained + s_pulse * delta(t, t_inject))

6.4  Excitatory/inhibitory decomposition

For Panel C (integration time constants), we need to separately
track excitatory and inhibitory signal propagation.  Implementation:

  # Create excitatory-only matrix (zero out inhibitory rows)
  signs = np.array([sign for _, sign in nt_sign_map.items()])
  excit_mask = signs > 0  # True for excitatory types
  inhib_mask = signs < 0  # True for inhibitory types

  W_excit = W.copy()
  W_excit[inhib_mask, :] = 0  # Zero out inhibitory presynaptic neurons

  W_inhib = W.copy()
  W_inhib[excit_mask, :] = 0  # Zero out excitatory presynaptic neurons

  # Propagate separately
  prop_excit = SignalPropagator(W_excit, nonlinearity="sigmoid_rectified")
  prop_inhib = SignalPropagator(W_inhib, nonlinearity="sigmoid_rectified")

  traj_excit = prop_excit.propagate_trajectory(s0, 6, sustained=False)
  traj_inhib = prop_inhib.propagate_trajectory(s0, 6, sustained=False)

Note: this decomposition is approximate because in reality, excitatory
and inhibitory signals interact at every step.  The decomposition shows
where each COMPONENT arrives, not where the NET signal lands.  This is
appropriate for Panel C's "arrival time" analysis.

6.5  Sensitivity analysis

All analyses should be repeated with:
  - Three nonlinearities: relu, sigmoid_rectified, leaky_relu
  - Two modes: pulse and sustained (for the pheromone component)
  - Two normalizations: post and pre (raw is too noisy for temporal
    analysis)

Results should be qualitatively consistent across parameter choices,
as demonstrated for the static analysis in Fig 6.
